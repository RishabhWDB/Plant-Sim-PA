\begin{abstract}
Digital twins are meant to support predictive decisions in agriculture, but the machine-learning
components inside them are usually validated only on pointwise error. We present a simulation
study of a hybrid plant-growth digital twin that couples an FAO-56-style soil water balance with
a learned growth-response surrogate and renders a 144-plant, seven-stage crop in Unity3D. We
study three prediction tasks: nowcasting the daily growth score, reproducing season outcomes, and
forecasting time to maturity. Comparing six regressors and eight training-data designs, we find
that pointwise test error is a poor guide to twin-level accuracy. The design with the worst
conventional test error (RMSE 0.031) gives the best simulated seasons
(99.0\% outcome agreement, 0.9-day maturity error) because it
samples the states the twin actually visits. The selected histogram gradient-boosting surrogate,
with isotonic calibration and split-conformal intervals, reaches $R^2 = 0.990$,
98.6\% accuracy in classifying growing versus deteriorating days, 90.4\% coverage of
90\% intervals, and a maturity-day error of 1.1 days over 400 seasons. Persisting
today's conditions forecasts maturity worse than climatology, whereas an ensemble of 30
stochastic weather futures run through the twin cuts the median error to 3 days
(42\% skill over climatology) with a Brier skill score of 0.64 for maturity within
the season. A day-by-day case study shows a dry spell pushing the crop through a critical
soil-moisture threshold, and a twin-triggered 35\,mm irrigation recovering
8 days. The surrogate runs natively in C\# as an exported tree ensemble.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

A digital twin is a virtual counterpart of a physical system that mirrors its state and lets
interventions be tested before they are applied~\cite{grieves2017digital}. In agriculture its value
is predictive: will the crop survive this dry spell, when will it mature, is irrigation worth
it now? Most reported agricultural twins, however, remain at the monitoring stage and depend on
dense sensing~\cite{verdouw2021digital,pylianidis2021introducing}.

A common way to give a twin predictive power cheaply is to replace an expensive process model
with a learned surrogate~\cite{razavi2012review}. Surrogates are normally judged by held-out
error, but inside a twin their outputs are integrated day after day. A bias that is negligible
pointwise can therefore accumulate into a large error in season length or outcome. The training
data are also typically sampled by design (uniformly, or stratified by hand) rather than from the
conditions the twin will actually meet, a form of dataset shift~\cite{quinonero2009dataset}.

We study these issues in a sensor-free plant-growth twin whose ground truth is a known process
model, which lets us measure exactly how surrogate errors propagate. We address three research
questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1.} How should training data for a twin's surrogate be designed, and does
        pointwise test error predict accuracy at the level of simulated seasons?
  \item \textbf{RQ2.} Which model, calibration and uncertainty method give accurate, calibrated
        daily nowcasts that can run inside a game engine?
  \item \textbf{RQ3.} How well can the twin forecast time to maturity, and what is needed to beat
        persistence and climatology baselines?
\end{itemize}

Our contributions are: (i) a hybrid twin combining a cardinal-range growth response with an
FAO-56-style water balance, implemented in Python and Unity3D; (ii) a comparison of six
regressors with five-fold cross-validation, conformal intervals and trajectory-level validation
on 400 simulated seasons; (iii) an ablation of eight training-data designs, showing that
\emph{operational sampling} of states visited by the twin removes the season-length bias that
pointwise metrics miss; (iv) a probabilistic ensemble forecaster of crop maturity evaluated
against persistence, moving-average and climatology baselines with proper scores; and (v) a
fully worked day-by-day case study.

\section{Related Work}
% =============================================================================

\textbf{Digital twins and crop models.} Verdouw \emph{et al.}~\cite{verdouw2021digital} propose a
reference architecture for smart-farming twins and Pylianidis
\emph{et al.}~\cite{pylianidis2021introducing} identify the lack of predictive layers as an open
problem. Process models such as DSSAT~\cite{jones2003dssat} and AquaCrop~\cite{steduto2009aquacrop}
simulate crop response mechanistically. They express factor effects through response curves:
cardinal temperatures~\cite{yan1999equation}, water-stress coefficients~\cite{allen1998crop},
radiation use~\cite{monteith1977climate}, and limitation by the scarcest
resource~\cite{paris1992von}.

\textbf{Surrogates and tabular learning.} Surrogate models emulate expensive simulators for
fast what-if analysis~\cite{razavi2012review}. Machine learning is widely used for crop
prediction~\cite{liakos2018machine,kamilaris2018deep}; on tabular inputs, random
forests~\cite{breiman2001random} and gradient boosting~\cite{friedman2001greedy}, including
histogram-based variants~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}, remain competitive with deep
networks~\cite{grinsztajn2022tree}. Training distributions that differ from deployment
distributions degrade predictive performance~\cite{quinonero2009dataset}.

\textbf{Calibration and uncertainty.} Isotonic regression corrects miscalibrated ensemble
outputs~\cite{zadrozny2002transforming,niculescu2005predicting}. Split-conformal prediction
gives distribution-free intervals with guaranteed marginal coverage~\cite{vovk2005algorithmic,
angelopoulos2023conformal}.

\textbf{Forecasting.} Probabilistic forecasts are evaluated with proper scoring rules such as the
Brier score~\cite{brier1950verification,gneiting2007strictly}, against reference forecasts such as
persistence and climatology. Ensemble forecasting propagates uncertainty in future conditions by
running a model under many plausible futures~\cite{leutbecher2008ensemble}; stochastic weather
generators supply such futures for crop models~\cite{richardson1981stochastic}.

% =============================================================================

the ground truth: a soil water balance and a growth response function $f$. Offline, we sample

\section{Process Model}\label{sec:process}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Growth Response Function}
The crop responds to $\mathbf{x} = (x_{\mathrm{pH}}, \theta, x_{\mathrm{sun}}, x_T, x_{\mathrm{RH}})$:
soil pH, soil moisture $\theta$ (\% of saturation), sunlight (h/day), temperature
(\textdegree C) and relative humidity (\%). Rainfall is \emph{not} a direct input; it acts
only through $\theta$. Each variable $j$ has an optimal band $[a_j, b_j]$, hard limits
$[l_j, u_j]$ and a weight $w_j$ (Table~\ref{tab:ranges}). With $m_j$ and $h_j$ the centre and
half-width of the band and $d_j$ the normalised distance outside it, the factor score is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi}
\phi_j(x) =
\begin{cases}
0 & x \le l_j \ \text{or}\ x \ge u_j \\[2pt]
0.85 + 0.15\left(1 - |x-m_j|/h_j\right) & a_j \le x \le b_j \\[2pt]
0.85\left(1 - d_j^{1.8}\right) & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
with $d_j = (a_j - x)/(a_j - l_j)$ below the band and $(x - b_j)/(u_j - b_j)$ above it. The curve
is continuous, leaves the band with zero slope ($\partial\phi_j/\partial d_j = -1.53\,d_j^{0.8}$),
and falls steeply near the hard limits, the plateau-then-decline shape of cardinal-temperature
models~\cite{yan1999equation} and of the FAO-56 stress coefficient~\cite{allen1998crop}. Solving
$\phi_j = c$ gives the threshold distance $d^*(c) = (1 - c/0.85)^{1/1.8}$; for soil moisture a
factor becomes \emph{stressed} ($\phi < 0.5$) below 18.6\% and \emph{critical} ($\phi < 0.2$)
below 9.8\%.

Factors are combined by a weighted mean $r = \sum_j w_j\phi_j / \sum_j w_j$ and two penalties. A
soft law of the minimum~\cite{paris1992von} applies when the weakest factor is critical,
$c_{\mathrm{crit}} = 0.5(\phi_{\min}/0.2)^{1.5}$ if $\phi_{\min} < 0.2$ and 1 otherwise, and a
compound-stress penalty $c_{\mathrm{comp}} = 0.6^{\max(0, n_s - 1)}$ applies when $n_s$ factors
are stressed. Seedlings are more vulnerable than established plants; with field stage
$k \in \{0,\dots,6\}$ (seed, germination, sprout, seedling, vegetative, flowering, mature) and
sensitivity $\sigma_k = 1.0, 0.9, \dots, 0.4$, the daily growth score is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:s}
s = f(\mathbf{x}, k) = \operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\big((r\,c_{\mathrm{crit}}\,c_{\mathrm{comp}})^{\gamma_k}\big),
\quad \gamma_k = 1 + 0.8\,\sigma_k .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Soil Water Balance}
Soil moisture is a state updated daily. With $W_t = \kappa\,\theta_t$ the root-zone water (mm),
\begin{align}
W_t &= \operatorname{clip}\!\big(W_{t-1} + P_t + I_t - ET_{c,t} - D_t,\; 0,\; 100\kappa\big), \label{eq:wb}\\
ET_{c,t} &= K_c(k)\,K_s(\theta_{t-1})\,ET_{0,t}, \\
K_s(\theta) &= \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\theta - \theta_{\mathrm{wp}}}{\theta_p - \theta_{\mathrm{wp}}}, 0, 1\right), \\
D_t &= 0.5\,\max\!\big(0,\, W' - \kappa\,\theta_{\mathrm{fc}}\big),
\end{align}
where $P_t$ is rainfall, $I_t$ irrigation and $W'$ the water before drainage. Following
FAO-56~\cite{allen1998crop}, transpiration is unrestricted until a fraction $p = 0.40$ (tomato) of
the available water between field capacity $\theta_{\mathrm{fc}} = 70\%$ and wilting point
$\theta_{\mathrm{wp}} = 5\%$ is depleted, i.e.\ $\theta_p = 44\%$, and the crop coefficient
$K_c$ follows the FAO-56 tomato values (0.60 initial, 1.15 mid-season, 0.80 late). Reference
evapotranspiration uses the Turc equation~\cite{turc1961evaluation}, which includes a humidity
correction, with Angstr\"{o}m radiation from sunshine hours $n$~\cite{allen1998crop}:
\begin{align}
ET_0 &= 0.013\,\frac{T}{T+15}\,(23.8856\,R_s + 50)\,c_{\mathrm{RH}}, \\
R_s &= \Big(0.25 + 0.5\,\frac{n}{N}\Big)R_a,
\end{align}
where $c_{\mathrm{RH}} = 1 + (50 - \mathrm{RH})/70$ when $\mathrm{RH} < 50\%$. Table~\ref{tab:wb} lists the
parameters. At 25\,\textdegree C, 62\% humidity and 8\,h of sunshine, $ET_0 = 4.37$\,mm/day;
in the hot, dry, sunny conditions of the case study it rises to 6.47\,mm/day.

\subsection{Crop Dynamics}
Each plant $i$ has progress $p_{i,t} \in [0,1]$ and a fixed offset
$\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{U}(-0.05, 0.05)$ representing natural variation. The field stage is
$\bar k_t = \min(6, \lfloor 7\bar p_t \rfloor)$. Each day
\begin{align}
p_{i,t} &= \operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\big(p_{i,t-1} + \Delta(\tilde s_{i,t})\big),\\
\tilde s_{i,t} &= \operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}(\hat s_t + \varepsilon_i),\\
\Delta(s) &= \begin{cases} 0.04\,(s - 0.5) & s \ge 0.5\\ -0.03\,(0.5 - s) & s < 0.5,\end{cases}\label{eq:delta}
\end{align}
where $\hat s_t$ is the score used by the twin. A score of 0.5 is neutral; the fastest possible
season is $\lceil 0.98/0.02 \rceil = 49$ days; a crop at progress $p$ survives at least $p/0.015$
days of total failure. The field \emph{matures} when $\bar p \ge 0.98$ and \emph{dies} when
$\bar p$ returns to zero after exceeding 0.05. Under persistence (today's score continues), the
days remaining are $T_t = (0.98 - \bar p_t)/\Delta(\hat s_t)$ to maturity, or $\bar p_t/|\Delta(\hat s_t)|$
to death.

\subsection{Weather Generator}
For validation and forecasting we generate rainfed seasons with a Richardson-type
generator~\cite{richardson1981stochastic}: rain occurrence follows a first-order Markov chain with
$P(\text{wet}\mid\text{dry}) \in [0.05, 0.45]$ and $P(\text{wet}\mid\text{wet}) \in [0.30, 0.80]$,
wet-day amounts are exponential with mean 3--20\,mm, temperature is an AR(1) anomaly
($\phi = 0.7$, $\sigma = 1.2$\,\textdegree C) around a site mean of 20--37\,\textdegree C, and
humidity and sunshine depend on whether the day is wet. Each season draws its own climate and
soil pH (5.2--7.6). Over 400 validation seasons the true process model gives 227
maturities, 11 deaths and 162 seasons that end immature.

\section{Predictive Modelling of the Growth Score}\label{sec:model}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Task, Data and Protocol}
The nowcasting task is regression of $s$ on six inputs (five variables and stage). Labels are
$f(\mathbf{x},k)$ with Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.012$). The final training set combines three
sources of 24{,}000 samples in total: 12{,}000 uniform samples over the full input space,
8{,}000 \emph{twin states} (the environment and stage on days the twin actually simulates in 600
independent rainfed seasons), and 4{,}000 samples near the optimal bands, 30\% of them within
$\pm 20\%$ of the band centres. It is split into 16{,}320 training, 2{,}448 isotonic-calibration,
1{,}632 conformal-calibration and 3{,}600 test samples.

Every model is assessed in three ways. \emph{Pointwise}: five-fold cross-validated RMSE on the
training split and $R^2$, RMSE and zone accuracy (correctly predicting $s \ge 0.5$) on the test
split. \emph{Probes}: the score at the optimum, where $f = 1$, and the fraction of the true
stage effect captured under mild stress. \emph{Trajectory-level}: the twin is run with the model
in place of $f$ on 400 held-out rainfed seasons, and compared with the true twin on outcome
agreement (matured, died or immature), maturity-day error and stress-day error.

\subsection{Model Comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:models} compares six candidates, each followed by isotonic calibration except the
linear model. A linear model cannot represent the plateau-and-cliff response. The tree ensembles
are all accurate pointwise, but they differ at the level of seasons. The random forest has a
competitive test error yet a 3.2-day maturity error. Deviation features (distance of each
variable below and above its band centre, normalised by the hard limits) give no material gain,
and monotonic constraints, which are physically correct for $f$, \emph{increase} test RMSE
from 0.036 to 0.068. The constrained booster enforces monotonicity greedily at
each split and cannot represent the interacting penalties well.

We tuned the histogram booster over learning rate $\{0.05, 0.1\}$, leaves $\{31, 63, 127\}$ and

\subsection{Training-Data Design (RQ1)}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} trains the selected model on eight designs of equal size and evaluates
all of them on the \emph{same} mixture test set, an \emph{operational} test set of twin states,
and the same 400 seasons. Three findings stand out. First, hand-stratified designs that
oversample stressed and shoulder conditions look reasonable on probes but carry a 2--5-day
maturity bias. Second, uniform sampling beats them at the trajectory level but underestimates the
optimum (0.931), which matters whenever the crop is well managed. Third, adding twin
states and a band-centre core removes both problems: zero maturity bias, 99.0\%
outcome agreement and an optimum of 0.999. Yet this design has the \emph{worst}
conventional test RMSE. Pointwise error on a designed test set ranks the designs almost in reverse
order of how well they serve the twin. Error on the operational test set agrees much better with
the trajectory metrics.

\subsection{Calibration, Uncertainty and Classification (RQ2)}
Fig.~\ref{fig:ml}(b) shows the reliability of the selected model before and after isotonic
calibration. On this training design the raw booster is already close to the diagonal, but it is
S-shaped around the decision threshold, overestimating the 0.3--0.4 bin and underestimating the
0.4--0.5 bin by up to 0.035. Isotonic calibration reduces the expected calibration error
(count-weighted mean gap over bins) from 0.0058 to 0.0017; its largest remaining gap
(0.058) lies in a bin of only 15 samples. At the trajectory level the two are
equivalent (outcome agreement 98.8\% without calibration and 98.0\% with it), so
calibration matters for the reliability of the daily nowcast near the threshold rather than for
season totals. For uncertainty we use split-conformal
prediction~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal}. The 90\% quantile of absolute residuals on the
1{,}632 conformal samples is $q = 0.044$, and the interval $\hat s \pm q$ covers
90.4\% of test targets, matching the nominal 90\%. Classifying a day as growing when
$\hat s \ge 0.5$, the model is correct on 98.6\% of test samples; of its 51 errors,
28 mislabel a growing day as deteriorating and 23 the reverse.

\section{Deployment in Unity3D}
% =============================================================================

Unity's neural-network inference runtime does not execute the ONNX-ML tree-ensemble operators
produced by scikit-learn converters, so we export the fitted ensemble directly: 1{,}000 trees with
125{,}000 nodes, the baseline, the isotonic breakpoints and $q$, as a 6.1\,MB JSON file.
A short C\# class traverses the trees (left if $x \le$ threshold), adds the leaf values to the
baseline, and interpolates the isotonic map. A reference evaluator that mirrors the C\# logic
line by line reproduces scikit-learn's predictions on the full test set to within
$10^{-15}$. The engine evaluates the model once per simulated day for the whole field, so
inference cost is independent of the number of plants. The water balance \eqref{eq:wb} runs in
the same daily step. The soil-moisture slider now follows the state, and dragging it irrigates
or drains the field to that level. The JSON loads in \ph{0.4\,s} at start-up. A $12 \times 12$
field renders at \ph{58} frames per second on an Apple M1 laptop (Table~\ref{tab:perf}); frame time
is dominated by the high-polygon late-stage meshes, and a $50 \times 50$ field produced
frame-time spikes of about 1.5\,s in the Unity Profiler.

\subsection{Worked Example: Day 41 of the Rainfed Run}
At the start of day 41, $\theta = 10.08\%$ and the field stage is 3 (seedling,
$\gamma = 1.56$). There is no rain or irrigation; $T = 35$\,\textdegree C, RH $= 38\%$,
$n = 10$\,h.

\emph{Step 1: water balance.} $K_s = (10.08 - 5)/(44 - 5) = 0.130$, $ET_0 = 6.47$\,mm and
$K_c = 0.90$, so $ET_c = 0.90 \times 0.130 \times 6.47 = 0.758$\,mm. The soil holds
$1.35 \times 10.08 = 13.60$\,mm, falls to 12.84\,mm, and
$\theta = 12.84/1.35 = 9.51\%$.

\emph{Step 2: factor scores} \eqref{eq:phi}. $\phi_{\mathrm{pH}} = 1$ (band centre),
$\phi_{\mathrm{sun}} = 0.85$ (band edge), $\phi_T = 0.7323$, $\phi_{\mathrm{RH}} = 0.7527$, and
\[
\phi_\theta = 0.85\Big(1 - \big(\tfrac{40 - 9.51}{40 - 5}\big)^{1.8}\Big) = 0.1871,
\]
below the critical level of 0.2.

\emph{Step 3: weighted mean.}
$r = (1.0 + 1.4 \times 0.1871 + 1.2 \times 0.85 + 1.4 \times 0.7323 + 0.9 \times 0.7527)/5.9 = 0.6754$.

\emph{Step 4: penalties.} $c_{\mathrm{crit}} = 0.5(0.1871/0.2)^{1.5} = 0.4523$; only soil
moisture is stressed, so $c_{\mathrm{comp}} = 1$.

\emph{Step 5: stage exponent.} $s = (0.6754 \times 0.4523)^{1.56} = 0.157$. The day
before, with $\theta = 10.08\%$ still above the critical level, $s$ was 0.549.

\emph{Step 6: surrogate and update.} The surrogate predicts $\hat s = 0.155$ (90\% interval
0.110--0.199), so $\Delta = -0.03(0.5 - 0.155) = -1.04\%$ per day and the
field's mean progress falls from 44.58\% to 43.56\%. Under persistence the twin forecasts
death in $0.4356/0.0104 = 42.0$ days.

\subsection{Observations}
\textbf{Days 1--14.} Soil moisture oscillates between 51 and 67\% under the
three-day rain cycle; the score stays at 0.94--0.99 and the field grows at about
1.9\% per day.

\textbf{Days 15--30, drying.} Evapotranspiration rises to 4.9\,mm/day and soil moisture falls
by about 3\% per day. Temperature becomes the limiting factor ($\phi_T = 0.73$) and growth halves to
about 1\% per day. Below $\theta_p = 44\%$ the stress coefficient restricts transpiration, so the
soil dries ever more slowly. This is the physical behaviour that a scripted daily drop would miss.

\textbf{Days 31--40, slow stress.} Soil moisture crosses the stress level (18.6\%) on day
31; the score falls towards 0.55 and growth nearly stops. The persistence forecast
stretches past 150 days.

\textbf{Days 41--44, collapse.} Soil moisture crosses the critical level on day 41, the
law-of-minimum penalty applies and the score drops from 0.549 to 0.157 in one day. Progress
reverses at 1.0--1.4\% per day and the field regresses from seedling to sprout on day
42.

\textbf{Recovery.} Rain on day 45 restores the soil and the score; the rainfed field
matures on day 85, 33 days after the optimal field. The warning-triggered policy
irrigates 35\,mm on day 36, the day after the score first falls below 0.6, which lifts soil
moisture from 14.1\% back to 39.0\% before it reaches the critical level. It keeps the score
above 0.5 throughout and matures on day 77, 8 days earlier than rainfed and with no stress days.

\section{Forecasting Time to Maturity (RQ3)}\label{sec:forecast}

\subsection{Forecasters and Scores}
On 400 new rainfed seasons (246 matured, 15 died, 139 immature) we issue
forecasts on days 10, 20, \dots, 60 for every crop still growing. We compare four forecasters of
the days remaining to maturity: \emph{persistence} (today's score continues), a \emph{7-day moving
average} of the score, \emph{climatology} (the median season length of 99 days, minus the
days elapsed), and an \emph{ensemble} that runs the twin forward under 30 weather futures drawn
from the site's climate, starting from the current soil moisture and the spread of plant progress.
The ensemble yields a median, an 80\% interval (10th to 90th percentile) and a probability of
maturing within the 120-day season. Point forecasts are scored by absolute error for seasons
that matured; the probability by the Brier score~\cite{brier1950verification} and its skill
relative to the climatological maturity rate.

every issue day (median error 11.1 days): the weather that holds today rarely holds for the
rest of the season, so a dry day predicts an endless season and a perfect day an unrealistically

(8.5 vs.\ 8 days). The ensemble is best
at every issue day, and its error shrinks from 5 days on day 10 to 2
days on day 60 as the remaining uncertainty shrinks. Its mean absolute error is 42\% lower

0.64. The 80\% intervals cover 87\% of outcomes, slightly conservative.

around the true maturity day, while persistence swings with each wet or dry spell.

of seasons that did not die also had at least one such day. Stress days are a useful early alert,
but predicting death itself needs the probabilistic forecast.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\textbf{RQ1: sample where the twin goes.} A surrogate inside a twin is evaluated on a distribution
that the twin itself produces. Designs chosen for coverage (uniform, or stratified by hand) leave
the most frequently visited states under-represented, and the resulting bias is invisible in
pointwise metrics but accumulates day by day. Since the season length is
$T = 0.98/(0.04(\hat s - 0.5))$, its sensitivity $\partial T / \partial \hat s = -0.98/(0.04(\hat s - 0.5)^2)$
is about $-130$ days per unit score near $\hat s = 0.93$, so an optimum underestimated by 0.07
costs roughly nine days. Operational sampling, plus a dense core at the optimum, removes the
bias. We recommend validating twin surrogates on trajectories, or at least on an operational
test set, which in our results ranks designs consistently with the trajectory metrics.

\textbf{RQ2: accuracy, calibration and deployability.} A tuned histogram booster gives the best
accuracy and trajectory fidelity. Isotonic calibration reduces calibration error near the
decision threshold without changing season-level results, and split-conformal intervals achieve
their nominal coverage. Physically motivated monotonic constraints hurt, which shows
that domain knowledge must be checked empirically against how a learner can use it. Exporting
the ensemble as data rather than through an inference runtime made deployment simple and
exactly reproducible.

\textbf{RQ3: forecasting needs futures.} A twin that only extrapolates today's state is a poor
forecaster beyond a few days; persistence loses to the trivial climatology baseline. Running the
twin under an ensemble of plausible weather turns it into a calibrated probabilistic forecaster
with substantial skill. The twin's value for forecasting lies in simulating many futures,
not only in mirroring the present.

% =============================================================================
\section{Limitations and Future Work}
% =============================================================================

This is a simulation study: the ground truth is a process model designed from agronomic
principles, not field measurements, so the results describe how predictive methods behave
inside a twin rather than how well the twin predicts a real crop. The twin states used for
training come from the same weather-generator family as the validation seasons, so the gain from
operational sampling may be smaller under a different climate; the uniform component is retained
for coverage. The weather generator is not fitted to station data, the water balance uses a
single root-zone layer, and the crop model has no nutrient, pest or disease dynamics. The
ensemble forecaster currently runs in Python; Unity shows the persistence forecast with its
conformal band. Future work includes calibrating the process model and weather generator
against field and station records, quantile or distributional surrogates, and running the
ensemble inside the engine.

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

We built a hybrid plant-growth digital twin and used it to study three predictive-analytics
tasks. Training data sampled from the states the twin actually visits matters more than the
choice among strong tree ensembles. Pointwise test error can rank surrogates almost in reverse
order of their usefulness inside a twin. Calibration and conformal intervals make the nowcast
trustworthy. Ensemble simulation is what makes the twin a skilful forecaster of crop maturity.
The selected surrogate reproduces season outcomes in 98.0\% of cases with a 1.1-day
maturity error and runs natively in Unity3D. The ensemble forecaster predicts maturity with a
median error of 3 days.
